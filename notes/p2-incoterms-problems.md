# Incoterms Problems — Full Solutions
The **exact 12 cases** from *IL problems for practice final part 1* 📘 (Cases 1–10 are 5–10 marks; Problems 11 and 12 are 10 marks). The *Incoterms* PPT repeats three of them as "Problem 1, 2, 3" — Problem 1 = Case 5 (textiles, CPT), Problem 2 = Case 1 (vaccines, DAP), Problem 3 = Case 4 (engine parts, CIF). The PPT also ends with two questions, answered at the bottom. Theory: [Incoterms® 2020](ch02-incoterms.md).

**Answer pattern for every Incoterms case (scores full marks):** ① name the rule with place and version (e.g. *DAP Berlin DC, Incoterms® 2020*); ② quote the clue words from the case that point to it; ③ seller's vs buyer's tasks, costs and customs; ④ the exact risk-transfer point; ⑤ insurance; ⑥ why not the nearest alternative.

## Case 1: Pharmaceuticals Export (DAP) ⭐⭐⭐
An Indian pharmaceutical manufacturer exports temperature-sensitive vaccines to a hospital network in Germany. The shipment moves by road–air–road. The exporter agrees to transport the vaccines up to the hospital's distribution center in Berlin, but the buyer will handle customs clearance and import duties.

**Q1.1 Identify the most appropriate Incoterm.**
**DAP — Delivered at Place (Hospital DC, Berlin), Incoterms® 2020.** Clues: seller carries the goods **to the buyer's premises** (a D term), the buyer **clears import and pays duties** (so not DDP), nothing is said about the seller unloading (so not DPU), and the route is multimodal road–air–road (DAP works for any mode).

**Q1.2 Justify why this Incoterm is suitable for temperature-sensitive pharmaceutical products.**
- The **seller controls the whole cold chain** door-to-door — one party chooses the GDP-certified forwarder, the active/passive temperature-controlled packaging, the airline (CEIV Pharma), monitoring loggers and the transfer at each hand-over (road → air → road). Splitting the chain between two parties (as under FCA/CPT) creates gaps at hand-overs, where most excursions happen.
- **Risk stays with the seller until arrival in Berlin**, so the seller has every incentive to protect the temperature, and the hospital receives a product it has not had to bear transit risk on.
- The **buyer**, a German hospital network, is better placed to handle **EU import clearance, import VAT and any regulatory release (GDP/EMA requirements)** — so DAP is better than DDP for an Indian exporter.

**Q1.3 Explain the responsibilities of the seller and buyer.**
| Seller (Indian manufacturer) | Buyer (German hospital network) |
|---|---|
| Provide goods, commercial invoice, packing list, certificate of origin, GMP/batch certificates | Pay the price |
| Temperature-controlled export packaging and labelling | Unload at the Berlin DC |
| Pre-carriage by road to the Indian airport; export customs clearance (shipping bill, IEC, drug export licence) | Import customs clearance in Germany/EU; pay import duty and import VAT |
| Contract and pay main air carriage + on-carriage by road to Berlin DC | Obtain any import licences/regulatory clearances |
| Bear all risk of loss/damage until arrival (insurance is not compulsory, but the seller insures in its own interest) | Bear risk from the moment goods are placed at its disposal at the DC |
| Notify buyer of arrival; provide delivery document/POD | Take delivery; pay demurrage/storage if import clearance is delayed |

**Q1.4 At what point does risk transfer from seller to buyer?**
When the goods are **placed at the buyer's disposal on the arriving vehicle at the Berlin distribution centre, ready for unloading**. Unloading, and anything after, is at the buyer's risk.

## Case 2: Steel Machinery Export (FCA) ⭐⭐⭐
A machinery manufacturer in Pune sells industrial equipment to a buyer in South Korea. The buyer appoints an international freight forwarder to collect the cargo from the seller's warehouse and arrange the entire international shipment.

**Q2.1 Which Incoterm best fits this transaction?**
**FCA — Free Carrier (Seller's warehouse, Pune), Incoterms® 2020.** Clue: the **buyer's forwarder collects** at the seller's warehouse and arranges the whole international move — an F term with the delivery point at the seller's premises.

**Q2.2 Why is this term preferred over EXW for international containerized shipments?**
- **Export clearance**: under EXW the foreign buyer must clear Indian export — it usually cannot (no IEC, no Indian presence) — while under FCA the **seller clears export**, which it can easily do.
- **Loading**: under FCA at the seller's premises, the **seller loads** the container/truck with its own forklifts/cranes (safer, insured, it knows the cargo); under EXW the buyer's trucker must load at someone else's site.
- **Proof of export**: the seller gets the shipping bill/export evidence it needs for GST refund (zero-rated export) and duty drawback/RoDTEP; under EXW it may not.
- **Container practice**: FCA fits containers and multimodal transport; the 2020 version even allows an **on-board B/L** to be issued to the seller for L/C payment.

**Q2.3 Explain the responsibilities of both parties.**
| Seller (Pune manufacturer) | Buyer (South Korean importer) |
|---|---|
| Goods, invoice, packing list; export packing and marking | Nominate and pay the forwarder/carrier; tell seller the pick-up date |
| **Load** the goods onto the buyer's collecting vehicle at its warehouse | Pay all main carriage (road to port, ocean/air to Korea, on-carriage) |
| **Export customs clearance** and export duties/formalities | Insurance (optional, buyer's interest) |
| Bear risk until loaded on the buyer's vehicle | Bear risk from loading onward; Korean import clearance, duty, VAT |
| Provide proof of delivery; assist with documents | Take delivery at destination |

**Q2.4 Who bears the export clearance responsibility?**
The **seller** — under FCA the seller clears the goods for export (in India: shipping bill filed via ICEGATE under the seller's IEC).

## Case 3: Coffee Beans Export (FOB) ⭐⭐⭐
A coffee exporter in India sells 15 containers of coffee beans to a buyer in Italy. The buyer nominates the shipping line. The seller is responsible for transporting the containers to Mumbai Port and loading them onto the buyer's vessel.

**Q3.1 Identify the correct Incoterm.**
**FOB — Free On Board (Mumbai / Nhava Sheva port), Incoterms® 2020.** Clues: **buyer nominates the shipping line** (F term, buyer pays main freight), seller takes the goods to the port **and loads them onto the vessel**.
(*Exam note:* strictly, for containers ICC recommends **FCA Mumbai container terminal**, because the containers are handed over at the CY before loading. The case explicitly makes the seller responsible up to loading on board, so FOB is the expected answer; mention FCA as the better practice for a bonus mark.)

**Q3.2 Explain when the risk transfers.**
When the goods are **placed on board the vessel** nominated by the buyer at Mumbai port (or when the seller procures goods already so delivered). Damage before loading (e.g. a container dropped on the quay) is the seller's; damage at sea is the buyer's.

**Q3.3 Why is this Incoterm suitable for sea transport?**
FOB is a maritime-only rule built around the ship's rail/on-board point; the seller handles what it knows (inland haulage in India, export clearance, port handling and loading) and the buyer controls the ocean freight, vessel choice and insurance — useful for buyers who have contract rates with shipping lines. It is the traditional term for **commodity trade** (coffee is a classic FOB commodity) and gives the buyer control over the main carriage.

**Q3.4 Compare it with CIF in terms of insurance responsibility.**
| | FOB | CIF |
|---|---|---|
| Who arranges and pays marine insurance? | **Buyer** (optional; in its own interest, since risk is the buyer's at sea) | **Seller must** buy insurance for the buyer's benefit |
| Level of cover | Whatever the buyer chooses | Minimum **ICC (C)** for **110%** of the contract value |
| Who pays ocean freight? | Buyer | Seller |
| Risk transfer | On board at Mumbai | **Also** on board at Mumbai — the same point |
So the risk point is identical; the difference is who **buys** the insurance and freight. Under CIF the buyer gets a policy without arranging it but only basic cover; under FOB the buyer can buy better cover (ICC A).

## Case 4: Automobile Parts Export (CIF) ⭐⭐⭐
An automobile component manufacturer exports engine parts to Brazil. The seller agrees to arrange sea freight and marine insurance until Santos Port. After arrival, the buyer will clear customs and arrange inland transportation.

**Q4.1 Which Incoterm should be used?**
**CIF — Cost, Insurance and Freight (Santos Port), Incoterms® 2020.** Clues: sea transport, seller pays **freight and marine insurance** to the destination port, buyer clears import and arranges inland transport. (If the parts are containerised, **CIP Santos container terminal** is the modern equivalent.)

**Q4.2 Explain why insurance is important under this term.**
Under CIF the **risk passes to the buyer when goods are on board in India**, but the seller controls the voyage. The buyer bears the risk for the whole sea journey without having arranged the carriage — so the contract requires the **seller to insure for the buyer's benefit**: minimum cover ICC (C) for 110% of the CIF value, with the policy/certificate endorsed to the buyer so it can claim in Brazil. It also protects the bank in L/C transactions. Because ICC (C) covers only major perils (fire, sinking, stranding, collision, jettison, general average), the buyer should ask for ICC (A) or buy top-up cover. Brazil also assesses import duty on CIF value.

**Q4.3 What responsibilities remain with the buyer?**
Risk of loss/damage from loading on board in India; unloading charges at Santos (unless included in the freight contract); **Brazilian import clearance** (Siscomex registration, import licence/LI, duties: II, IPI, ICMS, PIS/COFINS); paying duties and taxes; inland transport from Santos to its plant; any additional insurance; taking delivery and paying the price.

**Q4.4 At which point does risk transfer?**
**On board the vessel at the port of shipment in India** — not at Santos. (Two critical points: risk at the Indian port, costs paid by seller to Santos.)

## Case 5: Textile Export (CPT) ⭐⭐⭐
A textile company exports garments from India to Spain using multimodal transportation. The seller pays the freight up to Madrid Inland Terminal, but the buyer agrees to arrange insurance.

**Q5.1 Identify the most suitable Incoterm.**
**CPT — Carriage Paid To (Madrid Inland Terminal), Incoterms® 2020.** Clues: **multimodal**, seller **pays freight** to a named inland destination, **buyer arranges insurance** (so not CIP).

**Q5.2 Differentiate this term from CIP.**
| | CPT | CIP |
|---|---|---|
| Carriage to named destination | Seller pays | Seller pays |
| Insurance | **No obligation** on either party (buyer insures itself) | **Seller must insure**: ICC (A) all risks, 110% of value |
| Risk transfer | At hand-over to the **first carrier** in India | Same — first carrier |
| Price quoted | Lower (no premium) | Higher (includes premium) |
| Suits | Buyers with their own (open/annual) cargo policy | High-value or fragile goods; buyers who want the seller to insure |

**Q5.3 Explain who bears transportation costs and who bears risk.**
- **Costs**: the **seller** pays export packing, export clearance and the **entire freight** (road/rail to Indian port or ICD, ocean, and rail/road to Madrid Inland Terminal). The buyer pays unloading at Madrid (unless included in the carriage contract), import clearance, duties/VAT and onward delivery.
- **Risk**: passes to the **buyer** when the seller **hands the goods to the first carrier** in India (e.g. at the ICD/CFS). So the buyer bears transit risk for nearly the whole journey even though the seller pays the freight — which is why the buyer arranges insurance.

**Q5.4 Why is this Incoterm useful in multimodal logistics?**
CPT is designed for **any mode or combination**: one through-freight contract (often a multimodal B/L or FIATA FBL) covers road, sea and rail legs. Risk passes at the first carrier, a clear point that does not depend on a ship's rail; the seller controls routing and gets better rates, while an experienced buyer saves by using its own cargo insurance. It also fits containerised cargo, unlike CFR.

## Case 6: Electronics Export (CIP) ⭐⭐⭐
A consumer electronics company exports laptops from Bengaluru to Singapore through road–air transportation. Since the products are expensive and fragile, the seller agrees to arrange comprehensive insurance during transit.

**Q6.1 Which Incoterm is most appropriate?**
**CIP — Carriage and Insurance Paid To (Singapore Changi Airfreight Centre / buyer's named place), Incoterms® 2020.** Clues: road–air (multimodal, not sea), seller pays carriage and **arranges comprehensive insurance**.

**Q6.2 Explain why this term is preferred over CPT.**
Laptops are **high value and fragile** and theft-prone; under CPT nobody is obliged to insure, and the risk passes to the buyer at Bengaluru. CIP obliges the seller to buy **all-risks (ICC A / Institute Cargo Clauses Air)** cover for 110%, giving the buyer comprehensive protection without arranging it — the course's recommended term for **high-value goods**. It also reassures banks (L/C) and makes the seller's offer more attractive.

**Q6.3 Discuss the seller's insurance obligation under Incoterms 2020.**
- Insure at the seller's cost, with reputable underwriters, **from the point of delivery (first carrier) to at least the named destination**.
- **Minimum cover: Institute Cargo Clauses (A)** — "all risks" subject to exclusions (or ICC Air for air cargo). This is the 2020 change; under 2010 CIP needed only ICC (C).
- **Minimum amount: 110% of the contract price** (CIP value + 10%), in the contract currency.
- Provide the buyer with the **policy or insurance certificate** so the buyer (who bears the risk) can claim directly.
- At the buyer's request and cost, add war/strikes cover (Institute War and Strikes Clauses).
- Parties may agree a lower cover, but must say so expressly.

**Q6.4 Explain the point of risk transfer.**
Risk passes when the seller **hands the laptops to the first carrier** — e.g. the trucking company collecting at Bengaluru (or the airline/forwarder at Kempegowda airport if the seller delivers there). The insurance then protects the buyer for the remaining road and air transit.

## Case 7: Agricultural Equipment (DPU) ⭐⭐⭐
An Indian company exports heavy agricultural machinery to Kenya. The seller agrees not only to transport the machinery to the buyer's logistics center but also to unload it using cranes arranged by the seller.

**Q7.1 Identify the correct Incoterm.**
**DPU — Delivered at Place Unloaded (Buyer's logistics centre, Kenya), Incoterms® 2020.** Clue: the seller **unloads** at destination — the only rule with this obligation.

**Q7.2 What makes this Incoterm different from DAP?**
| | DAP | DPU |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery | On the arriving vehicle, **ready for unloading** | **Unloaded** from the arriving vehicle |
| Who unloads | Buyer | **Seller** |
| Risk transfer | Before unloading | **After unloading** |
| 2010 equivalent | DAP | DAT (Delivered at Terminal) |
Both are D terms: seller carries to destination and clears export; buyer clears import and pays duty.

**Q7.3 Explain the unloading responsibility.**
The **seller** must organise and pay for unloading at the Kenyan logistics centre — here, hiring and operating the cranes, riggers and lifting gear — and **bears the risk of damage during unloading**. It must make sure the place can physically accept the cargo (ground strength, access for cranes) before agreeing to DPU. The buyer must provide access to the site.

**Q7.4 Why is this Incoterm appropriate for oversized cargo?**
Heavy machinery needs **specialised lifting equipment and expertise**; the manufacturer knows the machine's lifting points, weight distribution and handling instructions. Making the seller responsible for unloading avoids disputes over damage caused during the riskiest operation (the lift), puts the job with the party that has the know-how and equipment contracts, and gives the buyer a complete delivered-and-landed service.

## Case 8: Solar Panels Export (DDP) ⭐⭐⭐
A solar equipment manufacturer exports solar panels to a customer in Canada. The exporter agrees to arrange transportation, insurance, customs clearance, payment of import duties, and delivery to the buyer's warehouse.

**Q8.1 Which Incoterm is applicable?**
**DDP — Delivered Duty Paid (Buyer's warehouse, Canada), Incoterms® 2020.** Clue: seller pays **import duties** and clears **import customs** — only DDP.

**Q8.2 Explain why this term places the maximum obligation on the seller.**
The seller does **everything**: export packing, export clearance, all carriage to the buyer's door, (voluntary) insurance, **import clearance in Canada, import duties and taxes (customs duty, GST/HST, any anti-dumping duties on solar products)**, and bears **all risk** until the goods are ready for unloading at the buyer's warehouse. It is the mirror image of EXW.

**Q8.3 What responsibilities remain with the buyer?**
Unloading at its warehouse; taking delivery; paying the price; providing information and assistance the seller needs for Canadian import clearance (e.g. business number, end-use details); bearing risk after delivery. (Insurance in this case is arranged by the seller though DDP does not oblige it.)

**Q8.4 Discuss one advantage and one disadvantage of this Incoterm for exporters.**
- **Advantage**: most **customer-friendly** offer — the buyer gets a landed, duty-paid price, which wins sales against competitors (common in e-commerce and B2C); the exporter controls the whole delivery experience.
- **Disadvantage**: **high cost and risk uncertainty** — the exporter must know Canadian customs (CBSA), may need a non-resident importer registration and GST/HST account, bears duty changes (e.g. new tariffs or anti-dumping/countervailing duties on solar panels) and clearance delays/storage costs, and may be unable to recover import GST as a foreign company.

## Case 9: Fresh Fruits Export (CFR) ⭐⭐⭐
A fruit exporter ships refrigerated containers of fresh grapes to Dubai by sea. The exporter agrees to pay ocean freight to Jebel Ali Port but does not arrange cargo insurance.

**Q9.1 Identify the appropriate Incoterm.**
**CFR — Cost and Freight (Jebel Ali Port), Incoterms® 2020.** Clues: sea, seller pays **freight to the destination port**, **no insurance** by seller (so not CIF). (For reefer containers, **CPT Jebel Ali** is the multimodal alternative.)

**Q9.2 Differentiate this term from CIF.**
Same as CIF in every respect — seller pays freight to Jebel Ali, risk passes on board at the Indian port, buyer clears import — **except insurance**: under **CFR** the seller has no duty to insure; under **CIF** the seller must buy ICC (C) cover for 110% of value for the buyer.

**Q9.3 When does the transfer of risk occur?**
When the grapes are **loaded on board the vessel at the Indian port of shipment** (e.g. Nhava Sheva/Mundra). Spoilage at sea from a reefer failure is the **buyer's** risk (the buyer then claims from its insurer or the carrier).

**Q9.4 Why might buyers choose to arrange their own insurance?**
- They hold an **annual/open cover** policy that is cheaper per shipment.
- They want **better cover** than CIF's minimum ICC (C) — perishables need ICC (A) with **refrigeration machinery breakdown** cover (ICC Frozen Food clauses), which ICC (C) does not include.
- Claims are easier with a **local insurer** in Dubai (local currency, local surveyors).
- Control over the insurer's rating and claim handling; can bundle with storage/inland cover.
- It lowers the price paid to the seller (no premium plus seller margin).

## Case 10: Industrial Chemicals Export (EXW) ⭐⭐⭐
A chemical manufacturer sells industrial chemicals to a multinational company. The buyer collects the goods directly from the seller's factory and manages loading, export documentation, transportation, customs clearance, insurance, and delivery.

**Q10.1 Which Incoterm best fits this transaction?**
**EXW — Ex Works (Seller's factory, named address), Incoterms® 2020.** Clue: the buyer collects **at the factory** and does **everything, including loading and export documentation**.

**Q10.2 Explain why this Incoterm gives minimum responsibility to the seller.**
The seller's only obligations are to **make the goods available at its premises**, suitably packed, at the agreed time, with the invoice, and to assist (at the buyer's risk and cost) with export information. It does **not load** the goods, does not clear export, pays no freight or insurance, and its risk ends when the goods are placed at the buyer's disposal — before loading.

**Q10.3 Discuss the challenges of using this Incoterm in international trade.**
- **Export clearance**: the foreign buyer generally **cannot be the exporter of record** in India (needs IEC, local registration); the seller ends up doing it anyway, outside the Incoterm.
- **Loading risk**: the buyer's truck crew loads at the seller's site — safety issues (hazardous chemicals!), insurance gaps, and the seller usually loads anyway "as a courtesy" but at the buyer's risk, which creates disputes.
- **No proof of export** for the seller → GST refund/zero-rating and drawback problems; possible tax liability.
- **Dangerous goods compliance**: chemicals need correct classification, DG packaging, labelling and MSDS; the buyer may not know Indian DG rules.
- **Security & screening, VGM** (verified gross mass for containers) and documentation responsibilities are unclear.
- The buyer carries the most cost and risk and must have strong logistics capability.

**Q10.4 Why do many exporters prefer FCA instead of EXW?**
Under FCA (seller's premises) the **seller loads** (with its own equipment, safely) and **clears export** (it is the natural exporter of record and gets export proof for tax refunds), while the buyer still controls and pays the main carriage. FCA keeps the low-responsibility spirit of EXW but removes its legal and practical problems; ICC itself recommends FCA over EXW for international sales.

## Problem 11: Choosing the Best Incoterm (10 Marks) ⭐⭐⭐
A food processing company in India exports ready-to-eat meals to three international buyers. **Buyer A (Japan)**: wants the seller to arrange freight and insurance only until Tokyo Port. **Buyer B (Germany)**: wants delivery to its warehouse but will pay import duties. **Buyer C (Australia)**: wants the seller to bear every responsibility, including import duties and customs clearance.

**Q11.1 Recommend the most appropriate Incoterm for each buyer.**
| Buyer | Incoterm |
|---|---|
| A — Japan | **CIF Tokyo Port**, Incoterms® 2020 (or **CIP Tokyo port terminal** if containerised) |
| B — Germany | **DAP Buyer's warehouse, Germany**, Incoterms® 2020 |
| C — Australia | **DDP Buyer's warehouse, Australia**, Incoterms® 2020 |

**Q11.2 Justify your recommendations.**
- **A — CIF**: seller pays **freight and insurance to the destination port** and nothing beyond; the buyer handles Japanese import (food safety checks under the Food Sanitation Act), duties and inland transport. Risk passes on board in India. Because ready-to-eat meals travel in containers, CIP is the technically better term; both satisfy "freight and insurance until Tokyo Port".
- **B — DAP**: seller delivers **to the warehouse** (a D term, door delivery) but **buyer pays import duties** and clears import, so not DDP; buyer unloads, so not DPU.
- **C — DDP**: "every responsibility including import duties and customs clearance" is the definition of DDP.

**Q11.3 Compare the seller's risk and cost under all three Incoterms.**
| | CIF (A) | DAP (B) | DDP (C) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seller pays | Export clearance, origin charges, ocean freight to Tokyo, minimum insurance | Everything to buyer's warehouse door: main + on-carriage, destination terminal handling | Everything in DAP **plus** import clearance, Australian duties and GST, biosecurity inspection fees |
| Seller's risk ends | **On board vessel in India** (earliest) | On arrival at buyer's warehouse, ready for unloading | Same as DAP, but also bears risk of **customs/biosecurity delay, rejection and duty changes** |
| Seller's cost level | Lowest of the three | Medium–high | Highest |
| Seller's risk level | Lowest | High | **Highest** |
| Customs knowledge needed | Indian export only | Indian export + transit to Germany | Indian export **and** Australian import (DAFF biosecurity, ICS) |

**Q11.4 Which transaction exposes the exporter to the highest operational risk? Explain.**
**Buyer C (DDP Australia).** The seller bears transit risk all the way to the door **and** the import process: Australia has some of the world's strictest **biosecurity and food import rules** (Department of Agriculture, Fisheries and Forestry — Imported Food Inspection Scheme, ingredient/meat restrictions, labelling under FSANZ). Any non-compliance can mean holding, testing, treatment, re-export or destruction at the seller's cost, plus demurrage and storage. The seller also pays duties and GST it may not recover, and needs an import agent/customs broker in Australia. Shelf-life of ready-to-eat meals makes delay especially costly.

## Problem 12: Wrong Incoterm Selection (10 Marks) ⭐⭐⭐
An exporter of precision medical equipment signs a contract using FOB, even though the shipment is transported by road–air. During air transportation, the cargo is damaged. The buyer argues that the seller should bear the loss, while the seller claims the responsibility ended after handing over the goods to the carrier.

**Q12.1 Was FOB the appropriate Incoterm? Justify your answer.**
**No.** FOB is a **sea and inland waterway only** rule; its delivery point is "on board the **vessel** at the named **port** of shipment". In a road–air move there is no vessel and no port of shipment, so the FOB delivery point **does not exist**. ICC explicitly says FOB is inappropriate for goods handed to a carrier before loading (containers, air). The contract therefore has an unclear risk-transfer point.

**Q12.2 Which Incoterm should have been used?**
- **FCA (named place, e.g. seller's factory or the airline's cargo terminal), Incoterms® 2020** — if the buyer arranges and pays the air freight. Risk passes on hand-over to the buyer's carrier, which is exactly what the seller believes happened.
- **CPT / CIP (named destination airport)** — if the seller pays the freight; **CIP** is best for **precision medical equipment** (high value, fragile) because the seller must insure all-risks (ICC A / ICC Air) for 110% for the buyer.
- **DAP** — if the buyer wants the seller to bear transit risk to its premises.
Under FCA, CPT or CIP, damage during the air leg would clearly be the **buyer's** risk (and under CIP the buyer would simply claim on the seller-provided policy).

**Q12.3 Explain how the incorrect Incoterm created confusion regarding risk transfer.**
- **Seller's view**: it handed the goods to the carrier (truck/airline), which is the "FCA logic", so risk passed then.
- **Buyer's view**: FOB means risk passes only when goods are "on board the vessel"; since there was no vessel, it never passed — or passes only when loaded on the aircraft at the named place, or even later — so the seller still bore the risk when the damage happened.
- The named place in FOB is a **port**, so the contract may name an airport or city that does not match the rule; each side reads the rule to suit itself. Insurance may also be missing or wrongly assigned, because each party assumed the other was at risk. The damage could have happened in the trucking leg, at the airport warehouse or in flight — with FOB, none of these maps to a defined delivery point. The result: a dispute that must go to negotiation or arbitration, delayed payment, and possibly the insurer refusing a claim from a party without insurable interest.

**Q12.4 Suggest measures that companies should adopt to avoid such contractual disputes.**
1. **Choose the Incoterm by transport mode** — never FAS/FOB/CFR/CIF for air, road, rail or containers; use FCA/CPT/CIP/DAP/DPU/DDP.
2. Write the rule fully: **rule + precise named place + "Incoterms® 2020"**.
3. **Insurance clause**: specify who insures, the cover (ICC A/Air), the amount (110%) and the period (warehouse-to-warehouse).
4. Align the Incoterm with the **payment terms and documents** (L/C, AWB, packing, inspection).
5. Train sales, logistics and finance staff on Incoterms; use a contract checklist/review by logistics before signing.
6. For high-value/fragile goods, agree **packaging standards, handling instructions and pre-shipment inspection** with photos at hand-over.
7. Add a **dispute-resolution clause** (arbitration, governing law, CISG applicability) and allocation of other risks Incoterms do not cover.
8. Obtain a clean receipt (AWB/forwarder's cargo receipt) at the hand-over point to prove condition at delivery.

## Questions at the end of the Incoterms PPT
**Q13. What INCOTERMS should you use?**
Choose by: (1) **mode** — sea-only terms (FAS, FOB, CFR, CIF) only for goods loaded directly on a ship (bulk, break-bulk); all-mode terms (EXW, FCA, CPT, CIP, DAP, DPU, DDP) for containers, air, road, rail and multimodal; (2) **who controls the main carriage** — buyer → E/F, seller → C/D; (3) **where risk should pass** — origin (C, F) or destination (D); (4) **insurance** — seller insures under CIF (ICC C) or CIP (ICC A); (5) **customs ability** — the party that can best clear export/import does so (avoid EXW for exports, DDP if the seller cannot clear import). Defaults: **FCA** for containerised exports, **CIP** for high-value goods, **FOB** for bulk commodities, **DAP** when the seller delivers and the buyer clears import, **DDP** for maximum buyer convenience.

**Q14. Classify INCOTERMS based on their mode of transport.**
**Any mode or modes (7)**: EXW (Ex Works), FCA (Free Carrier), CPT (Carriage Paid To), CIP (Carriage and Insurance Paid To), DAP (Delivered at Place), DPU (Delivered at Place Unloaded), DDP (Delivered Duty Paid). **Sea and inland waterway (4)**: FAS (Free Alongside Ship), FOB (Free on Board), CFR (Cost and Freight), CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight).
