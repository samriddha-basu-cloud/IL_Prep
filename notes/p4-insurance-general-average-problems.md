# Insurance & General Average Problems — Full Solutions
The **exact problems** from the course file *"Insurance and contract problems.docx"* 📘 — Que 1 to Que 7 (General Average) and Que 9 (cargo insurance claim); the file has no Que 8. The file gives the questions and value tables only, so every calculation and explanation below is a worked model answer. Theory: [Marine Insurance & General Average](ch06-marine-insurance-general-average.md).

## Method you will use every time ⭐⭐⭐
1. **Total GA loss** = all GA sacrifices + all GA expenditures.
2. **Total contributory value (CV)** = ship + all cargo (+ freight at risk, if given).
3. **GA rate** = GA loss ÷ CV × 100.
4. **Contribution** of each party = its value × GA rate.
5. **Net settlement** = amount made good to that party − its contribution (+ receives, − pays). Net positions add to **zero**.

> **Who is "made good" what?** The cargo owner whose goods were sacrificed is made good the value of the sacrifice. The **shipowner** is made good the **expenditures it paid** (firefighting, towage, salvage, repairs, security), because it paid them on behalf of the whole adventure. Showing this full settlement table earns the top marks.
> **Valuation note:** the given cargo values are used as contributory values (as the questions intend). Strictly, under YAR Rule XVII contributory value = arrived sound value + amount made good; mention this in one line if asked.

## Que 1. Fire on a Container Ship — MV Pacific Star (Singapore → Rotterdam) ⭐⭐⭐
The vessel catches fire; to stop it spreading, the captain orders several containers flooded with seawater. Extraordinary sacrifices and expenses: electronics damaged by seawater $300,000; firefighting expenses $150,000; emergency towing $250,000. The shipowner declares General Average.

| Particulars | Value ($) |
|---|---|
| Ship value | 9,000,000 |
| Electronics cargo | 2,500,000 |
| Textile cargo | 1,500,000 |
| Furniture cargo | 1,000,000 |

**Q1.1 Calculate the total General Average loss.**
GA loss = 300,000 (seawater damage to electronics — sacrifice) + 150,000 (firefighting) + 250,000 (towing) = **$700,000**.

**Q1.2 Determine the General Average contribution percentage.**
Total contributory value = 9,000,000 + 2,500,000 + 1,500,000 + 1,000,000 = **$14,000,000**.
GA rate = 700,000 ÷ 14,000,000 × 100 = **5%**.

**Q1.3 Calculate the contribution of the ship owner, electronics, textile and furniture cargo owners.**
| Party | Value ($) | × 5% = Contribution ($) |
|---|---|---|
| Ship owner | 9,000,000 | **450,000** |
| Electronics cargo owner | 2,500,000 | **125,000** |
| Textile cargo owner | 1,500,000 | **75,000** |
| Furniture cargo owner | 1,000,000 | **50,000** |
| **Total** | 14,000,000 | **700,000** ✔ |

**Q1.4 Determine the final settlement payable to the electronics cargo owner.**
Electronics owner is made good its sacrifice $300,000 and pays its contribution $125,000:
Net = 300,000 − 125,000 = **$175,000 receivable** by the electronics cargo owner.

Full settlement (check):
| Party | Made good ($) | Contribution ($) | Net ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ship owner (paid firefighting + towing) | 400,000 | 450,000 | **pays 50,000** |
| Electronics | 300,000 | 125,000 | **receives 175,000** |
| Textile | 0 | 75,000 | pays 75,000 |
| Furniture | 0 | 50,000 | pays 50,000 |
| **Total** | 700,000 | 700,000 | **0** ✔ |

## Que 2. Cyclone in the Arabian Sea — wheat jettisoned ⭐⭐⭐
To stabilise the vessel the captain orders part of the wheat thrown overboard. Losses: wheat jettisoned $250,000; tug assistance $120,000; emergency repairs $180,000.

| Item | Value ($) |
|---|---|
| Ship | 7,000,000 |
| Wheat | 1,800,000 |
| Rice | 1,200,000 |
| Sugar | 2,000,000 |

**Q2.1 Calculate the total GA loss.**
GA loss = 250,000 + 120,000 + 180,000 = **$550,000**.

**Q2.2 Calculate the contribution percentage.**
CV = 7,000,000 + 1,800,000 + 1,200,000 + 2,000,000 = **$12,000,000**.
GA rate = 550,000 ÷ 12,000,000 × 100 = **4.5833%** (≈ 4.58%).

**Q2.3 Calculate the liability of each stakeholder.**
| Party | Value ($) | Contribution @ 4.5833% ($) |
|---|---|---|
| Ship | 7,000,000 | **320,833.33** |
| Wheat | 1,800,000 | **82,500.00** |
| Rice | 1,200,000 | **55,000.00** |
| Sugar | 2,000,000 | **91,666.67** |
| **Total** | 12,000,000 | **550,000.00** ✔ |

**Q2.4 Calculate the compensation payable to the wheat owner.**
Net compensation = jettison made good − own contribution = 250,000 − 82,500 = **$167,500**.
(Shipowner: made good 120,000 + 180,000 = 300,000; contribution 320,833.33 → **pays $20,833.33** net. Rice pays 55,000; sugar pays 91,666.67. Net check: +167,500 − 20,833.33 − 55,000 − 91,666.67 = 0 ✔.)
*Note:* emergency repairs count as GA here because they were needed for the common safety (YAR Rule XIV — temporary repairs to allow the voyage to be completed); permanent repairs of accidental damage would be particular average.

## Que 3. Pirate Attack — Gulf of Aden ⭐⭐⭐
The captain hires private security and an emergency naval escort. Expenses: security payment $200,000; naval escort $150,000; hull repairs $250,000. No cargo was sacrificed, so every stakeholder simply contributes according to the GA percentage.

| Item | Value ($) |
|---|---|
| Ship | 10,000,000 |
| Machinery | 3,000,000 |
| Chemicals | 2,000,000 |
| Steel | 1,000,000 |

**Q3.1 Calculate the total contributory value of the ship and all cargo.**
CV = 10,000,000 + 3,000,000 + 2,000,000 + 1,000,000 = **$16,000,000**.

**Q3.2 Determine the total General Average loss.**
GA loss = 200,000 + 150,000 + 250,000 = **$600,000**.

**Q3.3 Calculate the General Average contribution percentage.**
GA rate = 600,000 ÷ 16,000,000 × 100 = **3.75%**.

**Q3.4 Calculate the GA contribution payable by (a) the ship owner, (b) machinery, (c) chemicals and (d) steel cargo owners.**
| Party | Value ($) | Contribution @ 3.75% ($) |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Ship owner | 10,000,000 | **375,000** |
| (b) Machinery | 3,000,000 | **112,500** |
| (c) Chemicals | 2,000,000 | **75,000** |
| (d) Steel | 1,000,000 | **37,500** |
| **Total** | 16,000,000 | **600,000** ✔ |
The shipowner paid all $600,000, contributes $375,000 itself, and **receives $225,000** from cargo (112,500 + 75,000 + 37,500).

**Q3.5 Explain why the expenses for private security, naval escort and hull repairs qualify as General Average expenses.**
- **Real and imminent common peril**: an armed pirate attack threatens the ship, crew and every cargo (hijack, ransom, total loss).
- **Extraordinary**: armed guards and a naval escort are not part of ordinary voyage costs.
- **Intentional and reasonable**: the master deliberately incurred them as a prudent response.
- **For the common safety**, and **successful** — ship and cargo were saved.
- **Hull repairs** qualify where they are repairs of damage caused by the GA act (e.g. evasive manoeuvres, defensive measures) or temporary repairs needed for the common safety to complete the voyage (YAR Rule XIV); permanent repair of damage caused by the pirates' gunfire itself would usually be particular average — so state that the question treats them as GA. (Ransom payments are also commonly treated as GA by English courts — *Masefield v Amlin*, 2011.)

**Q3.6 If the machinery cargo owner has a Marine Cargo Insurance Policy, explain how the GA contribution would typically be settled.**
The average adjuster asks for **GA security** before releasing the machinery: the owner signs a **GA bond**, and its **cargo insurer issues a GA guarantee** (instead of a cash deposit of $112,500). The cargo is released promptly. When the adjustment is final, the **insurer pays the $112,500 contribution** directly to the adjuster/shipowner — GA contributions are covered under ICC (A), (B) and (C) — subject to the policy limits (and proportionately if under-insured). The owner bears only any deductible.

**Q3.7 Differentiate between General Average and Particular Average with reference to this case.**
- **General Average**: the security payment, naval escort and (GA-qualifying) repairs were **deliberately incurred to protect everyone** from the pirates, so ship, machinery, chemicals and steel all share them at 3.75%.
- **Particular Average**: if, say, a pirate bullet had **accidentally** damaged one chemicals container, or the ship's own equipment was damaged by gunfire with no deliberate act for common safety, that loss would fall **only on its owner** (claimed from its own cargo or H&M insurer), not shared.

## Que 4. Engine Failure and Salvage — tanker in the Indian Ocean ⭐⭐⭐
The captain hires ocean salvage services and transfers part of the oil cargo to another vessel. Expenses: salvage $400,000; temporary cargo transfer $180,000; engine repairs $220,000. Oil transferred suffers damage worth $350,000.

| Item | Value ($) |
|---|---|
| Ship | 8,000,000 |
| Crude Oil | 5,000,000 |
| Lubricants | 1,500,000 |

**Q4.1 Calculate the total contributory value of the ship and cargo.**
CV = 8,000,000 + 5,000,000 + 1,500,000 = **$14,500,000**.

**Q4.2 Calculate the total General Average loss.**
GA loss = 400,000 + 180,000 + 220,000 + 350,000 (damage to crude during the transfer — a GA sacrifice) = **$1,150,000**.

**Q4.3 Determine the GA contribution percentage.**
GA rate = 1,150,000 ÷ 14,500,000 × 100 = **7.931%** (≈ 7.93%).

**Q4.4 Calculate the GA contribution payable by (a) the ship owner, (b) crude oil and (c) lubricants cargo owners.**
| Party | Value ($) | Contribution @ 7.931% ($) |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Ship owner | 8,000,000 | **634,482.76** |
| (b) Crude oil | 5,000,000 | **396,551.72** |
| (c) Lubricants | 1,500,000 | **118,965.52** |
| **Total** | 14,500,000 | **1,150,000.00** ✔ |

**Q4.5 Determine the net compensation payable (or receivable) by the crude oil cargo owner after adjusting for its GA contribution.**
Made good for damaged oil = $350,000; contribution = $396,551.72.
Net = 350,000 − 396,551.72 = **−$46,551.72** → the crude oil owner **pays a net $46,551.72**. (Its share of the common burden exceeds its own sacrifice because crude is a large share of the saved value.)

Full settlement:
| Party | Made good ($) | Contribution ($) | Net ($) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ship owner (paid salvage + transfer + repairs = 800,000) | 800,000 | 634,482.76 | **receives 165,517.24** |
| Crude oil | 350,000 | 396,551.72 | **pays 46,551.72** |
| Lubricants | 0 | 118,965.52 | **pays 118,965.52** |
| **Total** | 1,150,000 | 1,150,000 | **0** ✔ |

**Q4.6 Explain why salvage, temporary cargo transfer and emergency engine repairs qualify as GA expenses.**
- **Salvage operation**: with a total engine failure the tanker was drifting in peril (grounding, breaking up, pollution); salvage services were engaged **for the common safety** of ship and cargo — allowed in GA under YAR Rule VI.
- **Temporary cargo transfer (lightering)**: deliberately transferring oil to another vessel to lighten/stabilise the ship and preserve ship and cargo is an **extraordinary expenditure** for common safety (YAR Rules VIII/X), and damage to oil during the transfer is a **GA sacrifice**.
- **Emergency engine repairs**: temporary repairs carried out to enable the ship to proceed safely and complete the voyage for the benefit of all (YAR Rule XIV, limited to the saving of other GA costs). The breakdown itself is particular average; the emergency/temporary repair cost for the common safety is GA.
All are extraordinary, intentional, reasonable, for common safety and successful.

**Q4.7 If the ship owner carries Hull and Machinery (H&M) insurance and the cargo owners have Marine Cargo Insurance, explain how the losses and liabilities would generally be settled.**
- An **average adjuster** prepares the GA adjustment; cargo is released against **GA bonds + insurers' GA guarantees**.
- **H&M insurers** reimburse the shipowner's share of GA ($634,482.76) and its GA expenditures not recovered from cargo, plus particular-average damage to the engine (the breakdown repair beyond GA), subject to the deductible.
- **Cargo insurers** pay the crude and lubricants owners' **GA contributions** ($396,551.72 and $118,965.52) and indemnify the crude owner for its damaged oil (then recover via the GA adjustment by subrogation).
- If the breakdown resulted from the shipowner's failure to exercise due diligence (unseaworthiness), cargo insurers may refuse/recover contributions and the shipowner turns to its **P&I club**.

**Q4.8 Discuss the importance of General Average in protecting the interests of all parties involved in international shipping.**
It lets the master act **immediately** in an emergency (salvage, lightering) without negotiating with each owner; it **spreads the cost fairly** so the party whose property was sacrificed is not ruined while others benefit; it gives **legal certainty** through the York-Antwerp Rules in every B/L; it links neatly to **insurance**, so traders are protected; it protects the **environment and crew** by encouraging timely salvage; and it maintains confidence in shipping as a shared venture, especially on mega-ships carrying thousands of consignments.

## Que 5. Grounding of a Car Carrier ⭐⭐⭐
A vehicle carrier grounds in heavy fog. To refloat it, several luxury vehicles are removed using floating cranes. Extraordinary losses: vehicle damage $500,000; floating crane $250,000; towage $350,000; repairs $300,000.

| Item | Value ($) |
|---|---|
| Ship | 12,000,000 |
| Luxury Cars | 6,000,000 |
| Auto Parts | 2,000,000 |

**Q5.1 Calculate the total contributory value of the ship and cargo.**
CV = 12,000,000 + 6,000,000 + 2,000,000 = **$20,000,000**.

**Q5.2 Determine the total General Average loss.**
GA loss = 500,000 + 250,000 + 350,000 + 300,000 = **$1,400,000**.

**Q5.3 Calculate the GA contribution percentage.**
GA rate = 1,400,000 ÷ 20,000,000 × 100 = **7%**.

**Q5.4 Calculate the GA contribution payable by (a) the ship owner, (b) luxury car and (c) auto parts cargo owners.**
| Party | Value ($) | Contribution @ 7% ($) |
|---|---|---|
| (a) Ship owner | 12,000,000 | **840,000** |
| (b) Luxury cars | 6,000,000 | **420,000** |
| (c) Auto parts | 2,000,000 | **140,000** |
| **Total** | 20,000,000 | **1,400,000** ✔ |

**Q5.5 Calculate the net compensation receivable by the luxury car cargo owner after deducting its GA contribution.**
Net = 500,000 − 420,000 = **$80,000 receivable**.
(Shipowner: made good 250,000 + 350,000 + 300,000 = 900,000; contributes 840,000 → **receives $60,000**. Auto parts **pays $140,000** = 80,000 + 60,000 ✔.)

**Q5.6 Explain why floating crane charges, towage, emergency repairs and damage to vehicles during refloating are treated as GA.**
- **Floating crane charges** — hired deliberately to remove cargo and **lighten the grounded ship** so it could be refloated: extraordinary expenditure for common safety (YAR Rule VIII — expenses of lightening a stranded ship).
- **Towage** — to pull the ship off and to safety: extraordinary expenditure for common safety (salvage/towage, Rule VI).
- **Emergency repairs** — temporary repairs needed to make the ship safe to continue the voyage for everyone's benefit (Rule XIV).
- **Damage to the vehicles during refloating** — damage caused **by the GA act itself** (handling by cranes in an emergency) is a GA sacrifice (Rules VIII/C — direct consequence of the GA act).
In each case there was a real common peril (a grounded ship can break up), the act was intentional and reasonable, and ship and cargo were saved.

**Q5.7 Assume the luxury cars were not insured. Explain the financial consequences for the cargo owner.**
- It must provide **GA security itself** — usually a **cash deposit** (adjusters often ask for a high percentage of cargo value, e.g. 10–20% or more = $0.6–1.2 million) or a bank guarantee — before the carrier releases the cars; until then the carrier exercises a **lien**.
- Its cash is locked up until the adjustment is finalised (possibly years); it pays **storage, demurrage and delays**, and may lose sales/customers.
- It eventually receives its net $80,000 only after the adjustment and collection from others — and bears the risk of other parties failing to pay.
- Any **particular-average** damage (accidental, e.g. from the grounding impact itself) is unrecoverable, as are adjuster fees for its account and legal costs.
- If the cars had been insured (ICC A), the insurer would have posted a guarantee, paid the $420,000 contribution and the sacrifice, and the owner would have suffered almost nothing.

**Q5.8 Explain the role of the General Average Adjuster in this case and describe the steps followed in determining each party's liability.**
Role: an independent expert appointed by the shipowner to decide what is GA and to prepare the **adjustment** fairly for all parties under the YAR and the B/L.
Steps:
1. Receive the **GA declaration** and the casualty facts (master's report, logs, survey reports of grounding, cargo damage).
2. Collect **GA security** from each cargo interest (GA bond + insurer's guarantee or cash deposit) and commercial invoices.
3. Classify each loss as **GA or particular average** (crane, towage, repairs, vehicle damage → GA).
4. Establish **values**: ship's sound value at the end of the voyage, cargo arrived values (+ amounts made good), freight at risk → **contributory values** ($20 m).
5. Total the **GA loss** ($1.4 m), adding allowable commission and interest (YAR Rules XX–XXI).
6. Compute the **GA rate** (7%) and **each party's contribution**.
7. Prepare the **adjustment statement** with amounts made good and net balances (cars +80,000; ship +60,000; parts −140,000).
8. **Collect and distribute** balances, release security.

**Q5.9 From the perspective of international logistics, discuss why General Average clauses are included in Bills of Lading and marine insurance contracts.**
The B/L is the contract of carriage with every cargo owner; the GA clause binds all of them in advance to share GA under the **York-Antwerp Rules**, at a named place of adjustment, and gives the carrier a **lien for GA security** — essential when a ship carries thousands of consignments of different nationalities. Insurance contracts include GA/salvage cover (all ICCs) so traders are protected and cargo is released promptly against guarantees. Together they ensure **fair loss-sharing, quick emergency decisions by the master, predictability across legal systems, fast cargo release, and lower disputes** — supporting the reliability of global trade.

## Que 6. Refrigerated mangoes, Mumbai → Dubai (₹) ⭐⭐⭐
Machinery breakdown at sea; emergency power supply arranged to prevent total spoilage, costing **₹18,00,000** (GA expenditure). Ship value = ₹5 crore; total cargo value = ₹7 crore; freight at risk = ₹1.5 crore.

**Q6.1 Calculate the total contributory value.**
CV = 5 + 7 + 1.5 = **₹13.5 crore** (₹13,50,00,000).

**Q6.2 Determine the General Average contribution rate.**
GA rate = 18,00,000 ÷ 13,50,00,000 × 100 = **1.3333%** (1⅓%).

**Q6.3 Calculate the GA contribution payable by cargo owners.**
Cargo = ₹7,00,00,000 × 1.3333% = **₹9,33,333** (₹9.33 lakh).
(For completeness: ship ₹5 crore × 1.3333% = ₹6,66,667; freight ₹1.5 crore × 1.3333% = ₹2,00,000; total ₹18,00,000 ✔. The shipowner, who paid ₹18 lakh, recovers ₹9,33,333 from cargo; the freight share ₹2,00,000 is borne by whoever has the freight at risk — normally the carrier.)

## Que 7. General Average Contribution — automobile parts, Mumbai → Singapore (₹) ⭐⭐⭐
Engine-room fire; to save the voyage the master hires emergency towing costing **₹36,00,000**, declared GA. Ship value = ₹12 crore; total cargo value = ₹18 crore; freight at risk = ₹3 crore.

**Q7.1 Calculate the total contributory value.**
CV = 12 + 18 + 3 = **₹33 crore**.

**Q7.2 Determine the General Average contribution rate.**
GA rate = 36,00,000 ÷ 33,00,00,000 × 100 = **1.0909%** (≈ 1.09%).

**Q7.3 Calculate the GA contribution payable by cargo owners.**
Cargo = ₹18 crore × 1.0909% = **₹19,63,636** (₹19.64 lakh).

**Q7.4 Calculate the GA contribution payable by the shipowner.**
Ship = ₹12 crore × 1.0909% = **₹13,09,091** (₹13.09 lakh).
Freight at risk = ₹3 crore × 1.0909% = ₹3,27,273. If the freight at risk belongs to the shipowner (freight payable on delivery, earned by the carrier), the shipowner's total share = 13,09,091 + 3,27,273 = **₹16,36,364**. Check: 19,63,636 + 13,09,091 + 3,27,273 = ₹36,00,000 ✔.

## Que 9. Cargo insurance claim — 800 LED TVs, Chennai → Hamburg ⭐⭐⭐
CIF value per TV = ₹42,000. Rough seas damage 12% of the cargo. The marine policy covers 95% of the cargo value. Deductible = 3% of the total cargo value.

**Q9.1 Calculate the total value of the shipment.**
Total CIF value = 800 × 42,000 = **₹3,36,00,000** (₹3.36 crore).

**Q9.2 Calculate the value of cargo damaged.**
12% × 3,36,00,000 = **₹40,32,000** (96 TVs × 42,000).

**Q9.3 Determine the insurance claim payable.**
- Covered loss (95% of the damaged value) = 95% × 40,32,000 = ₹38,30,400.
- Deductible = 3% × total cargo value = 3% × 3,36,00,000 = ₹10,08,000.
- **Claim payable = 38,30,400 − 10,08,000 = ₹28,22,400.**

**Q9.4 Calculate the uninsured loss borne by the exporter.**
Uninsured loss = damaged value − claim = 40,32,000 − 28,22,400 = **₹12,09,600**
(= 5% uninsured portion ₹2,01,600 + deductible ₹10,08,000).

> **Alternative convention** (deductible applied before the 95% cover): (40,32,000 − 10,08,000) × 95% = ₹28,72,800 claim; uninsured loss ₹11,59,200. State your method. Also note: a policy covering only 95% of value is **under-insured** relative to the usual 110% of CIF; with 110% cover the exporter would have recovered the full damage less the deductible. Heavy-weather damage is covered under ICC (A) (all risks); ICC (B) covers it only where sea water entered the vessel/container, and ICC (C) does **not** cover it.
