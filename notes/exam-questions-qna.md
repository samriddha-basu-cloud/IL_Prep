## Outsourcing & contracts

**Q1. What is outsourcing? Discuss its advantages and risks in international logistics. (10 marks)**
Outsourcing is contracting an external provider to perform an activity the firm could do in-house; in logistics, transport, warehousing, forwarding, customs and even supply-chain design are outsourced to 2PL–5PL providers. **Advantages**: lower cost (scale, labour arbitrage, variable cost), focus on core competence, access to expertise and technology (WMS, cold chain, global networks), flexibility for peaks, faster market entry, capital release, risk sharing. **Risks**: loss of control and visibility, hidden costs (audits, integration, QC, governance), service failures, IP and data leakage, dependence and switching costs, regulatory and ethical exposure (labour practices), currency and political risk, cultural/communication gaps. **Managing them**: TCO-based business case, weighted supplier selection, robust contract (SLA, LDs, audit, exit), risk matrix and mitigation, governance and KPI scorecards, dual sourcing for critical services.

**Q2. Distinguish outsourcing, offshoring, nearshoring and insourcing.**
Outsourcing: a third party performs the activity (anywhere). Offshoring: the activity moves to another country (own captive unit or third party). Nearshoring: moving to a nearby country for shorter lead times and lower risk. Insourcing/reshoring: bringing activities back in-house/home.

**Q3. Explain the key clauses of a logistics outsourcing contract.**
Scope of work; price and indexation (fuel, currency); SLA with KPIs, service credits and incentives; liquidated damages; force majeure (definition, notice, mitigation, termination after a period); termination for cause (with cure period) and convenience; warranties and indemnities; limitation of liability; insurance; confidentiality and IP; audit rights; code of conduct/ESG; business continuity; change control; governance and escalation; dispute resolution and governing law; exit and transition-out; step-in rights.

**Q4. What is an SLA? How do you design one for an outsourced warehouse?**
A service level agreement defines measurable service levels, how they are measured and the consequences. Design: choose 5–8 KPIs (order accuracy, fulfilment time, inventory accuracy, damage rate, complaints, safety), define each formula and data source, set targets from baselines/benchmarks, specify measurement frequency and reporting, attach service credits for misses and bonuses for exceeding, list exclusions, set escalation and review meetings, and link repeated failure to termination rights.

**Q5. Explain hidden costs of outsourcing and why they are ignored.**
Hidden costs are the extra costs caused by outsourcing beyond the contract price: search and audit, travel, transition and integration, quality inspection and rework, contract management and governance, coordination, buffer inventory and exit. They are ignored because they fall in other budgets, arise later, are hard to estimate, and procurement focuses on price. Include them in TCO.

**Q6. Explain force majeure with reference to the COVID-19 lockdown.**
Force majeure excuses non-performance caused by events beyond a party's control, unforeseeable and unavoidable — e.g. a government-ordered lockdown — if the clause covers it, the party gives prompt notice with evidence and mitigates. It suspends obligations (no damages), and prolonged FM may permit termination. Post-COVID contracts name pandemics and government orders explicitly, require notice timelines, partial performance, allocation, and hardship/termination provisions.

**Q7. Single sourcing versus multi-sourcing: which builds resilience?**
Single sourcing gives lower cost, consistent quality and partnership but creates a single point of failure; multi-sourcing costs more and is harder to manage but spreads risk, adds flexible capacity and keeps competition. Resilience comes from multi-sourcing (geographically diverse), supported by safety stock and visibility; in stable times a primary + qualified backup split balances both.

**Q8. Define BATNA and explain its use in a freight contract negotiation.**
BATNA is the best alternative if no agreement is reached (e.g. spot market or another carrier for the buyer; other shippers for the carrier). It sets each side's walk-away point and bargaining power; parties explore trade-offs (volume vs price, speed vs price, contract length, indexation) within the zone of possible agreement.

## Incoterms

**Q9. What are Incoterms? Why are they important in international trade? (10 marks)**
Incoterms are the ICC's standard trade terms (first 1936, now 2020) that define the tasks, costs and risks of seller and buyer for delivery of goods: who arranges and pays each leg, who clears export and import, who insures, and where risk passes. Importance: clarity and certainty, fewer disputes and delays, lower costs, better risk management, simpler communication, alignment with L/Cs, insurance and customs valuation; globally recognised in all trade documents. Limits: they do not cover title, payment, price, remedies, FM, IP, tariffs or disputes.

**Q10. Classify the Incoterms 2020 and explain each briefly. (10 marks)**
Any mode: EXW (goods at seller's premises, buyer does everything); FCA (delivered to buyer's carrier, seller clears export); CPT (seller pays carriage to destination, risk at first carrier); CIP (CPT + seller insures ICC A 110%); DAP (seller delivers to named place ready for unloading; buyer clears import); DPU (seller delivers and unloads); DDP (seller delivers duty paid). Sea only: FAS (alongside ship); FOB (on board); CFR (on board, seller pays freight to destination port); CIF (CFR + seller insures ICC C 110%). Groups E, F, C, D in ascending seller obligation.

**Q11. "Risk passes before cost in C-terms." Explain with CIF.**
Under CIF the seller pays freight and insurance to the destination port, but risk passes to the buyer when goods are loaded on board at the port of shipment. Hence two critical points: risk at origin, costs at destination — which is why the seller must insure for the buyer's benefit.

**Q12. Why should FCA be preferred to EXW and FOB for containerised exports?**
EXW leaves export clearance and loading to a foreign buyer who usually cannot do them, and the seller gets no proof of export; FOB keeps risk with the seller until loading on board although containers are handed over at a yard/ICD days earlier and the seller no longer controls them. FCA passes risk on hand-over to the carrier, the seller clears export and loads at its premises, it works for all modes, and the 2020 on-board B/L option satisfies L/C requirements.

**Q13. What changed in Incoterms 2020?**
DAT renamed DPU; CIP insurance raised to ICC (A) while CIF stays at ICC (C); FCA on-board bill of lading option; use of own transport in FCA, DAP, DPU, DDP; security costs allocated; costs consolidated in A9/B9; article order changed; expanded guidance notes.

**Q14. What factors determine the choice of Incoterm?**
Mode of transport; who has better logistics capability and freight rates; risk appetite and where risk should transfer; insurance needs and cargo value; customs expertise and legal ability to clear export/import; payment method (L/C needs C-terms or FCA on-board B/L); competitiveness of the offer; cargo nature (bulk vs container, oversized, perishables).

## Containerization & imbalance

**Q15. Define containerization. Explain its objectives and advantages. (10 marks)**
(See chapter.) ISO-standard boxes carried across modes without rehandling goods. Objectives: weather protection, a standard box, easier loading/unloading, temporary storage, reduced theft and pilferage, crane handling by hooks, reduced handling. Advantages: lower port handling cost, faster ship turnaround, less damage and pilferage, intermodal transport, economies of scale, lower insurance, standardised equipment and documentation.

**Q16. Explain the different types of containers with examples.**
General cargo, thermal (reefer for meat/fish, insulated for fruit/veg with dry ice, ventilated for tea/coffee), dry (coal, grain, metals), flat rack (pipes, tanks, machinery), bulk (cement, ores, grain), garment (hanging clothes), tank (milk, chemicals), gas (liquid oxygen), open top (tall cargo), car carrier, special purpose (weapons), open side (long rail bars), double door, tunnel, half-height (dense cargo).

**Q17. What is container imbalance? Discuss its causes, effects and remedies. (10 marks)**
A spatial mismatch between container supply and demand: surplus empties in import-heavy regions (US, Europe), shortages in export-heavy regions (Asia, India). Causes: trade imbalance, high rates, space/weight issues, single forwarder for many destinations, container shortage, storage costs, demand surges, outdated planning, no carrier cooperation. Effects: late orders and cash-flow stress, higher freight and CIC, delays, stock-outs and penalties, environmental damage, unreliable trade. Remedies: digital tracking and forecasting, CAx monitoring, empty repositioning, virtual pools, SOC/one-way leasing, street turns, sharing, substitution, connectainers, foldable containers, optimisation models.

**Q18. Explain empty container repositioning and its costs.**
Moving empty boxes from surplus to deficit locations without cargo. Necessary because of trade imbalance, storage costs and time pressure. Costs: ship slot/freight, handling at both ends, drayage, transhipment, storage, repairs, planning — industry cost $15–20 bn/yr; empties are ~20.5% of port handlings.

**Q19. What is the Container Availability Index?**
Container xChange's real-time measure of container availability at a port/region (ratio of full containers entering vs leaving). 0.5 = balance; >0.5 surplus; <0.5 shortage; persistent readings guide repositioning and leasing decisions.

## Maritime costing & air security cost

**Q20. Explain the components of ocean freight and the factors affecting maritime freight rates. (10 marks)**
Components: base freight (per container FCL, per m³/t LCL), surcharges (BAF, CAF, PSS, EBS, GRI, security, green fees), handling (THC origin/destination), additional costs (CFS, documentation), service-based costs (inspection, customs, inland haulage, insurance, last mile). Factors: economic (supply–demand of ships/boxes, peak season, cycles), logistic (operating costs, routes, port infrastructure, handling capacity), regulatory/political (regulations, trade agreements, conflicts), environmental (emissions rules, weather, technology), plus fuel, port fees, currency, insurance, container type and special handling.

**Q21. Describe the methods of calculating freight rates with formulas.**
Per mile/km (rate × distance); weight-based (rate × weight); dimensional (L×W×H ÷ DIM factor; charge on higher of DIM and actual); flat rate; zone-based (zone pair rate); freight class for LTL (density = weight ÷ volume → NMFC class 50–500). Ocean: chargeable = max(actual, volumetric), 1 m³ = 1,000 kg; total = base + surcharges + extras.

**Q22. Explain the basic principles of air freight price calculation.**
Measure to extreme points (rectilinear box; protrusions charged); weight or measure — 6,000 cm³ = 1 kg (1 m³ = 166.67 kg); chargeable weight is the higher, rounded up to the next 0.5 kg; multiply by the agreed tariff for the weight break and route (minimum charge; rate per kg decreases with weight); add fuel and war-risk surcharges; add origin and destination charges (pick-up, handling, documentation, customs, screening, delivery); consider limited carrier liability (22 SDR/kg) and insure.

**Q23. Discuss air cargo security risks and measures, with key regulations.**
Risks: explosives/incendiaries, undeclared dangerous goods, contraband, theft, hijack/sabotage. Measures: screening, facility security, known shipper/regulated agent regimes, staff training, access control, tamper-proof seals, blast-resistant ULDs, biometrics. Regulations: EU 831/06 (2007), UK known/unknown cargo, TSA 100% screening on passenger aircraft (2012) after the 2010 Yemen bomb, EU screening at origin (2012), ACC3 and independent validation (2014), US–EU mutual recognition (2012).

## Insurance & GA

**Q24. Explain General Average with its essentials and an example. (10 marks)**
Definition, essentials (common peril, extraordinary, intentional, reasonable, common safety, success, direct consequence), York-Antwerp Rules, calculation (GA loss ÷ contributory values), settlement and security (GA bond, guarantee), example (jettison of wheat in a cyclone; flooding containers to fight fire), role of insurance (contributions covered under ICC A/B/C).

**Q25. Distinguish General Average from Particular Average.**
GA: intentional, for common safety, shared by all interests rateably, adjusted under YAR. PA: accidental partial loss borne by the owner of the damaged property, recovered from its own insurer.

**Q26. Explain the principles of marine insurance.**
Utmost good faith, insurable interest, indemnity, subrogation, contribution, proximate cause, warranties, mitigation of loss.

**Q27. Compare ICC (A), (B) and (C).**
A: all risks subject to exclusions (CIP minimum). B: named perils including C's plus earthquake/lightning, washing overboard, water entry, package lost in loading. C: fire/explosion, stranding/sinking, collision, overturning, discharge at port of distress, GA sacrifice, jettison (CIF minimum). All exclude wilful misconduct, inherent vice, insufficient packing, delay, war/strikes (unless added).

**Q28. What is the role of the General Average adjuster?**
Independent expert who determines GA items, collects GA security from cargo before release, values the ship, cargo and freight, computes contributions and the GA rate, prepares the adjustment statement and settles balances.

## Air transportation

**Q29. Explain the structure of the air cargo industry. (10 marks)**
IATA and trade associations (standards for safety, security, training, ULDs; services like CASS); airlines (own/lease aircraft; combination carriers, all-cargo, integrators); lessors (dry, damp, wet/ACMI); IATA cargo agents (freight forwarders issuing HAWBs); airport authorities (AAI in India) and cargo terminal operators; plus ICAO/DGCA/BCAS regulators and customs.

**Q30. Differentiate AWB and HAWB; explain consolidation.**
The AWB is the airline's contract of carriage with the shipper (or forwarder); the HAWB is issued by an IATA forwarder to each shipper whose cargo is consolidated; the MAWB covers the consolidated load between forwarder and airline. Consolidation combines many small shipments into one MAWB, getting lower weight-break rates; savings are shared between forwarder and shippers.

**Q31. Explain the types of air freight services and aircraft.**
Services: air mail, express (time-definite), scheduled, charter (heavy/bulky/unserved destinations), leased (dry, damp, wet/ACMI), forwarder services. Aircraft: passenger (belly), quick-change, combi, dedicated freighter.

**Q32. Describe how air freight works (15 steps).**
Supplier → Incoterms → forwarder → commercial documents → volumetric ratio → secure packing → route/airline → documentation → AWB after departure → transit → pre-arrival customs → arrival → terminal operator stores under bond → transport collection (tail-lift) → delivery.

**Q33. Discuss the advantages and limitations of air freight with suitable products.**
Advantages: speed, reliability, security, track & trace accuracy, control (less storage, perishability), scheduling; suitable for high-value, urgent, perishable goods (pharma, electronics, flowers, fish). Limitations: high cost (4–5× road, 12–16× sea), limited capacity, weather, risk, restrictions on dangerous goods and heavy machinery.

## KPIs

**Q34. Explain FOB terms and freight payment responsibilities (Table 11.4).**
Six combinations of FOB Origin/Destination with Freight Collect, Prepaid, Prepaid & Charged Back, Collect & Allowed; FOB point decides ownership and claims; payment term decides who pays the carrier; charged back/allowed shift the ultimate cost; control follows carrier selection.

**Q35. What are the common transportation KPIs and how is a carrier scorecard built? (10 marks)**
On-time delivery (> 95%), transit time average (low variation), damage rate (< 1%), shortage rate (< 1%), billing accuracy (> 99%), perfect delivery index (> 95%). Scorecard: criteria, weights, banded point scales, weight × points, total out of 500, compare carriers, review, reward or eliminate. Example Table 11.8 = 405.

**Q36. Compare the modes of transport on strengths, limitations and performance.**
Truck: accessible, fast, versatile; limited capacity, high cost. Rail: high capacity, low cost; accessibility, inconsistent service, damage. Air: speed, protection, flexibility; accessibility, high cost, low capacity. Water: capacity, low cost, international; slow, accessibility. Pipeline: in-transit storage, efficient, low cost; slow, limited network. Rankings per Table 11.6.

## Short notes (5 marks each)
**Q37. Write short notes on: (a) CIC (b) SOC (c) street turns (d) BAF/CAF (e) NVOCC (f) ULD (g) CASS (h) ACC3 (i) York-Antwerp Rules (j) DPU.**
(a) Container Imbalance Charge: surcharge to recover empty repositioning costs, paid by the party creating the imbalance. (b) Shipper-owned container: leased/owned by the shipper for one-way moves; avoids CIC, demurrage/detention and gives control. (c) Street turn: reusing an import box directly for a nearby export load. (d) BAF fuel and CAF currency adjustment surcharges. (e) NVOCC: carrier without ships that issues its own B/L, consolidates and resells space. (f) ULD: aircraft pallet/container shaped to the hold, IATA-coded. (g) CASS: IATA's settlement system — one payment a month. (h) ACC3: EU validation of non-EU carriers' cargo security at origin (2014). (i) YAR: international GA adjustment rules (latest 2016), incorporated in B/Ls. (j) DPU: Incoterm where the seller delivers and unloads at the named place; replaced DAT.
