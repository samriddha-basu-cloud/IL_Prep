## A. Basic principles of air freight price calculation ⭐⭐⭐ 📘

### Step 1 — Measure the cargo
Measure dimensions **in centimetres to the most extreme points** of each piece: **length × width × height = cargo volume**.
- **Cylindrical or unusually shaped items** are charged on the volume of the **box the item would fit inside exactly**, not the true volume — all calculations are **cubic and rectilinear**.
- If part of the cargo **sticks out** (a pipe, an aerial), the **entire additional cubic area** that the extension consumes is charged, however small the protrusion.

### Step 2 — Weight or measure (whichever is higher)
As in sea freight, air uses a **weight or measure** system: the airline charges on the **higher** of actual weight and volume weight. Industry relationship:
- **6 m³ = 1 metric tonne**, i.e. **6,000 cm³ = 1 kg**
- therefore **1 m³ = 166.67 kg** of chargeable weight.
- Some carriers use **5,000 cm³/kg** (common with couriers/express) — the method is the same, only the divisor changes.

### Step 3 — Rate × chargeable weight
Air freight = agreed **tariff rate per kg** × **chargeable weight**, which is **always rounded up to the next half kilogram**.
**Example (PDF):** 150 kg, 120 × 80 × 50 cm → volume 0.48 m³ → 0.48 × 167 = **80 kg** (or 120 × 80 × 50 ÷ 6,000 = 80 kg) → chargeable = **150 kg** (actual weight exceeds volume equivalent).

### Step 4 — Look up the tariff
Find the agreed rate for that chargeable weight from origin airport to destination airport on the **schedule of rates** from the forwarder or airline.

### Step 5 — Rate tariffs and weight breaks
Rates can be agreed in advance with forwarders or airlines. There is **always a minimum chargeable weight (minimum charge)**, and generally the **cost per kg falls as chargeable weight rises** (weight breaks). 📗 IATA/TACT structure: **M** (minimum charge), **N** (normal rate, under 45 kg), **Q45, Q100, Q300, Q500, Q1000** (quantity rates), plus **SCR** (specific commodity rates, lower, for named commodities on named routes) and **class rates** (surcharges or reductions as a % of N, e.g. live animals, valuables higher; newspapers, books lower). Always check whether a higher weight break at a lower rate gives a lower total ("**break-point**" rule).

### Step 6 — Surcharges
Airlines may add a **fuel surcharge**, **war-risk premium** and, today, a **security surcharge (screening)** depending on the destination and the surcharges in force at the time.

### Step 7 — Other charges outside the airport-to-airport rate ⭐⭐⭐
The rate covers only carriage **from one airport to another**. Also budget for:
- **At origin**: (i) transport from the consignor's premises to the forwarder's warehouse and on to the airport; (ii) **origin airport handling**; (iii) forwarder's **documentation** costs; (iv) **customs clearance** if applicable; (v) **security screening** if required.
- **At destination**: (i) **destination airport handling**; (ii) forwarder's documentation costs; (iii) **customs clearance**, which may include **duties and taxes**; (iv) security screening if required; (v) transport from the destination airport to the **consignee**.

### Carrier's liability and insurance ⭐⭐⭐ 📘
- The basic liability air carriers accept is **very limited**: the **Montreal Convention 1999 (revised 2019)** limits cargo liability to **22 Special Drawing Rights (SDR) per kg** (IMF publishes daily SDR values). At the time of the text, **1 SDR = US$1.43493**, so **22 SDR = US$31.56846 per kg**. (📗 Since 28 Dec 2024 the limit is 26 SDR/kg after ICAO's 5-yearly review. The older **Warsaw Convention 1929** limit was 250 gold francs ≈ 17 SDR/kg.)
- This may not cover many cargoes' value, so **insure the cargo in transit** — premium = a small percentage of the **declared value**; buy full **door-to-door** insurance, because the carrier's liability covers only the flight.
- Use an **IATA-registered cargo agent** experienced with the destination country or cargo type; check **restrictions** on what may be flown (dangerous goods).
- **Charters**: large consignments or a whole freighter need specialist charter agents; pricing is negotiated but uses the same principles; single charters are often priced on a **round-trip basis** and can be very expensive vs standard per-kg rates.

## B. Air cargo security ⭐⭐⭐ 📘

### Major risks
1. **Explosive or incendiary devices** placed inside air cargo before loading.
2. **Undeclared or undetected hazardous material** on board.
3. **Smuggling of contraband** inside air cargo.
4. **Theft** from air cargo.
5. **Hijacking or sabotage** by people with access to the aircraft.

### How the risks are limited
- Implementing or improving **cargo screening and inspection** (X-ray, explosive trace detection, sniffer dogs, physical search).
- Improving the **physical security of air cargo facilities**.
- **"Known shipper" programmes** (and regulated agents / known consignors).
- Improving **staff security training**.
- **Restricting access** to aircraft and air cargo facilities.
- Using the latest security technology: **tamper-proof seals**, **blast-resistant hard-sided cargo containers (HULDs)**, **biometric data** for personnel, improved **screening systems**.

### Key regulations and milestones ⭐⭐⭐
| Year | Rule | What it requires |
|---|---|---|
| **Jan 2007** | **EU Regulation 831/06** | Regulated agents and airlines must obtain specific factual information (including **financial information such as bank account details**) about clients consigning air cargo; those clients must allow **compliance inspectors to audit** them — replacing self-regulation, which the EU judged unsatisfactory |
| UK (DfT, 2009) | Screening rule | All cargo on domestic or international flights from the UK must be **screened** to a standard that reasonably ensures it carries no prohibited article (incendiary/explosive device); by the airline or a **regulated agent**; once screened, it must be stored and transported **securely** until loaded. **Unscreened = "unknown" cargo; screened and protected = "known" cargo** |
| **2010** | **Yemen printer-cartridge bomb plot** | A consignment from Yemen to the US was found to contain a bomb — triggered tighter rules |
| **From 2012** | **US TSA** | **All cargo loaded on passenger aircraft must be screened for explosives** (100% screening) |
| **Since 2012** | EU | All cargo **bound for the EU** must be screened **at origin** |
| **2012** | US–EU | **Mutual recognition** of each other's air cargo security regimes |
| **Since 2014** | EU **ACC3** (Air Cargo or Mail Carrier operating into the Union from a Third Country Airport) | Carriers serving the EU must be ACC3-compliant: an **EU national airline/authority inspects and approves** the security screening of non-EU airlines at origin; since 2014 all carriers' cargo security at departure airports is **inspected and approved by independent validators** (EU aviation security validation) |

📗 Related: ICAO **Annex 17** (security), the **secure supply chain** concept (regulated agent → known consignor → account consignor), **PLACI / ICS2** (pre-loading advance cargo information — data filed before loading so authorities can risk-assess), and in India the **BCAS** (Bureau of Civil Aviation Security) Regulated Agent regime.

### Why security raises cost ⭐⭐
Screening equipment, labour and time (security surcharges per kg), secure storage, validation/audits, training, data filing, delays for unknown cargo, and rejected or re-screened shipments. Known-shipper status lowers these costs — a trade-off between **security and speed/cost**.

## Self-test
**Q1. State the airline industry's weight–volume relationship.**
6 m³ = 1 tonne, i.e. 6,000 cm³ = 1 kg, so 1 m³ = 166.67 kg chargeable; some carriers use 5,000 cm³/kg.

**Q2. How is an unusually shaped item (a cylinder or a box with an aerial) measured?**
On the rectilinear box it fits in exactly, including the full cubic space consumed by any protrusion; true volume is not used.

**Q3. What charges apply beyond the airport-to-airport rate?**
Origin: pick-up and transfer to forwarder and airport, origin handling, forwarder documentation, export customs, security screening. Destination: airport handling, documentation, import customs with duties/taxes, screening, delivery to consignee.

**Q4. Why should air cargo be insured even though the airline is liable?**
Montreal Convention liability is limited to 22 SDR/kg (≈US$31.57/kg at the textbook rate), often far below the cargo value, and it covers only the flight; door-to-door insurance covers the full value and all legs.

**Q5. What are the five main air cargo security risks?**
Explosive/incendiary devices in cargo, undeclared hazardous materials, smuggling of contraband, theft, hijacking or sabotage by insiders.

**Q6. Explain "known" and "unknown" cargo.**
Known cargo has been screened (by an airline or regulated agent, or comes from a validated known consignor) and kept secure until loading; unknown cargo is unscreened and must be screened before it can fly.

**Q7. What is ACC3?**
The EU's designation for carriers flying cargo into the EU from third-country airports; since 2014 they must have their origin screening inspected/validated and approved, so EU-bound cargo is screened at origin to EU standards.
